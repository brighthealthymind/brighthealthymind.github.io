💛 Emotional Intelligence · Ages 7–10 · About 5 min

Name It to Tame It: Understanding Your Feelings

Feelings are like messages from the inside of you. Happy, sad, angry, scared, excited and worried are all feelings. Every feeling is okay to have. Even grown-ups feel all of them!

Your body gives you clues about how you feel. When you are worried, your tummy might feel tight or full of butterflies. When you are angry, your face might get hot and your hands might want to squeeze. Noticing these clues helps you figure out what you are feeling.

Here's a cool trick: when you say the name of your feeling, like "I feel frustrated," that big feeling often starts to shrink a little. That's why people say "name it to tame it."

When a feeling is really big, try this: Stop. Breathe. Talk. Stop what you are doing. Take slow breaths, in through your nose like smelling a flower and out through your mouth like blowing out a candle. Then talk to someone you trust. Feelings are always okay, and we get to choose what we do with them.

🔍 Thinking Skills · Ages 7–10 · About 5 min

Think Like a Detective

Critical thinking means thinking carefully before you decide what is true. Detectives don't guess. They look for clues first!

Try the detective steps: 1. Stop and don't rush. 2. Ask questions like "How do I know?" and "Is there proof?" 3. Look for clues and evidence. 4. Decide on the answer that matches the clues best.

It also helps to know the difference between a fact and an opinion. A fact can be checked and shown to be true, like "A dog has four legs." An opinion is what someone thinks or feels, like "Dogs are the best pets." Opinions are fine, but they aren't the same as facts.

When you hear something surprising, like an ad saying a toy will make you "the smartest kid ever," a good thinker asks: "How do they know? What's the proof?"

🦁 Story with a Moral · Ages 7–10 · About 5 min

The Lion and the Mouse

A retelling of a classic fable by Aesop.

One sunny afternoon, a big lion was sleeping under a tree. A tiny mouse was scurrying by and accidentally ran right across the lion's nose! The lion woke up and caught the mouse under his giant paw.

"Please let me go!" squeaked the mouse. "If you are kind to me, maybe one day I can help you." The lion laughed. How could a tiny mouse ever help a mighty lion? But he decided to be kind, and he let the mouse go.

A few days later, the lion got trapped in a hunter's net. He roared and pulled, but the ropes only got tighter. The little mouse heard the roar and came running. She chewed and chewed through the ropes until the lion was free!

"You laughed at me," said the mouse, "but now you know that even a little mouse can help a lion." The moral: kindness is never wasted, and nobody is too small to help.

🌍 Science Basics · Ages 7–10 · About 5 min

Why Do We Have Day and Night?

Every morning the Sun seems to rise, and every evening it seems to set. It looks like the Sun is moving across the sky, but here's the secret: it's the Earth that is moving!

The Earth is always spinning, like a slow spinning top. It makes one full spin about every 24 hours, and that's what we call one day.

The Sun shines on only one half of the Earth at a time. The half facing the Sun has daytime. The half facing away from the Sun has nighttime. As the Earth spins, your home turns into the sunlight (morning) and later turns away from it (evening).

That means when it's daytime where you live, it's nighttime for kids on the other side of the world! Try it at home: shine a flashlight on a ball and slowly turn the ball to see day and night move.

🍕 Math · Ages 7–10 · About 6 min

Fractions Are Just Fair Sharing

Imagine you and a friend share a pizza. To be fair, you cut it into 2 equal pieces. Each piece is one half, written as 1/2. A fraction is a way of showing equal parts of a whole.

Now imagine four friends sharing one pizza. You cut it into 4 equal pieces. Each piece is one quarter (or one fourth), written as 1/4.

Here's how to read a fraction. The bottom number tells how many equal parts the whole is cut into. The top number tells how many of those parts you have. So 3/4 means the pizza was cut into 4 equal parts, and you have 3 of them.

Notice something? When a pizza is cut into more pieces, each piece is smaller. So 1/4 of a pizza is smaller than 1/2 of the same pizza. And 2 quarters make 1 half. The word "equal" is very important: fractions are all about fair sharing!

🐷 Life Skills · Ages 7–10 · About 5 min

Money Basics: Needs vs. Wants

Money helps families buy the things they need and some of the things they want. But what's the difference?

Needs are things we must have to live and stay healthy and safe, like healthy food, clean water, a home, clothes for the weather and care from a doctor when we're sick.

Wants are things that are nice to have but we can live without, like toys, video games, candy or fancy light-up sneakers. Wants are fine! But smart money choices take care of needs first.

When you want something that costs more than you have, you can save a little at a time until you reach your goal. Some kids use three jars: Save, Spend and Share (for helping others). Waiting and saving for something makes getting it feel even better!

🤝 Emotional Intelligence · Ages 7–10 · About 5 min

Walking in Someone Else's Shoes

Have you ever heard someone say, "Try walking in someone else's shoes"? They don't mean really swapping shoes! It means imagining how another person feels. This is called empathy.

You can be a feelings detective for other people. Look at their face and body: are they smiling, frowning or sitting alone? Listen to their words and their voice. Then imagine: "How would I feel if that happened to me?"

Imagine a classmate drops their lunch tray and everyone turns to look. They might feel embarrassed. With empathy, you might help pick things up or say, "That happens to everyone. Want some help?"

Empathy doesn't mean you have to fix everything. Sometimes the kindest thing is just to listen and say, "That sounds hard. I'm here." Small acts of kindness can make a big difference to someone's day.

🌱 Thinking Skills · Ages 7–10 · About 5 min

Mistakes Help Your Brain Grow

Did you know your brain can grow stronger, a bit like a muscle? When you practice something, the connections inside your brain get stronger. That's how hard things slowly become easier!

Believing you can get better with effort and practice is called a growth mindset. Think about learning to ride a bike. At first you wobble and maybe fall. With practice, you ride without even thinking about it.

Mistakes are not a sign that you're "bad" at something. Mistakes show you what to practice next. Every time you figure out what went wrong and try again, your brain is learning.

Try adding the magic word "yet". Instead of "I can't do fractions," say "I can't do fractions yet." That tiny word reminds you that you're still learning, and that's exactly how everyone gets better.

🐢 Story with a Moral · Ages 7–10 · About 5 min

The Tortoise and the Hare

A retelling of a classic fable by Aesop.

Once upon a time, a speedy hare loved to show off. "I'm the fastest animal in the forest!" he bragged. "Nobody can beat me!" A tortoise, who moved slowly but surely, said calmly, "Let's have a race." The hare laughed so hard his ears flopped.

The race began. Zoom! The hare shot ahead and soon the tortoise was far behind. "This is too easy," the hare yawned. "I have plenty of time for a nap." He lay down under a shady tree and fell fast asleep.

Meanwhile, the tortoise kept going, one step at a time. He didn't stop and he didn't give up. Step, step, step. He walked right past the sleeping hare.

When the hare finally woke up, he dashed toward the finish line, but it was too late. The tortoise had already crossed it, and all the animals were cheering! The moral: slow and steady wins the race. Keep going, don't give up, and don't show off.

🌻 Science Basics · Ages 7–10 · About 5 min

Why Do Plants Need Sunlight?

People and animals get energy by eating food. But plants can't go to the kitchen for a snack! Instead, plants do something amazing: they make their own food.

To make food, a plant needs three things: sunlight, water (which it drinks up through its roots) and carbon dioxide, a gas in the air that goes in through tiny holes in its leaves.

Leaves are green because they contain something called chlorophyll. Chlorophyll catches sunlight and helps turn water and carbon dioxide into sugar, the plant's food. This is called photosynthesis (foe-toe-SIN-thuh-sis).

Here's the best part: while making food, plants give off oxygen, the gas that people and animals need to breathe. That's one reason plants are so important! Try it: if a plant is kept in a dark closet for a while, it can turn pale and weak because it can't make enough food.

🌋 Emotional Intelligence · Ages 7–10 · About 6 min

What to Do When You're Angry

Everybody feels angry sometimes. Anger is a normal feeling. It often shows up when something feels unfair, when we get hurt, or when things don't go our way.

Anger has body clues too: a hot face, a fast heartbeat, tight fists or a loud voice. Think of an anger thermometer. Noticing your anger while it's still small makes it easier to cool down before it boils over.

Try a cool-down trick: take slow, deep breaths, count slowly to 10, get a drink of water, squeeze a pillow, or move to a calm spot for a few minutes. One important rule: it's okay to feel angry, but it's not okay to hurt people, yourself or things.

When you feel calmer, use your words with an "I statement": "I feel angry when ___ because ___." For example: "I feel angry when you take my markers because I was using them." Calm words help people listen and solve the problem together.

🕵️ Thinking Skills · Ages 7–10 · About 6 min

Is It True? Spotting Tricks Online

The internet is full of fun videos, games and facts. But here's something important: not everything online is true. Anyone can post things, and pictures and videos can be edited to look real.

Before you believe something, ask three detective questions: Who made this? Does it sound too good to be true? Can I check it with a trusted source, like a grown-up, a teacher or a good book?

Watch out for tricks like pop-ups that say "You won a prize! Click here!" These are usually trying to get you to click or share information. Don't click. Tell a trusted grown-up.

Keep your private information private. Never share your full name, home address, school, phone number or passwords with strangers online. And if anything online makes you feel worried, scared or uncomfortable, it's always okay to tell a trusted grown-up. You won't be in trouble for asking for help.
