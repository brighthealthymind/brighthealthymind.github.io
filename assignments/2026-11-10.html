Solids, Liquids, and Gases: The States of Matter

Read the lesson together, take the 5-question quiz, then hold an ice-melting race at home.

🧊 Science · Ages 7–10 · About 10 min

Everything Is Made of Matter

Your chair, the juice in your cup, and the air you breathe are all made of matter. Matter is anything that takes up space. Here on Earth, most matter comes in three common forms, called states: solid, liquid, and gas.

Tiny particles. All matter is made of particles far too small to see. In a solid, like a wooden block or an ice cube, the particles are packed tightly and only wiggle in place, so a solid keeps its own shape. In a liquid, like water or milk, the particles are still close together but can slide past each other, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of whatever container it is in. In a gas, like the air in a balloon, the particles are far apart and zoom around, so a gas spreads out to fill all the space it can.

Changing states. Heating or cooling can change matter from one state to another. When ice warms up, it melts into liquid water. When water gets very hot, it boils and turns into an invisible gas called water vapor. When water vapor cools down, it condenses back into tiny drops, like the fog on a bathroom mirror. And when liquid water gets cold enough, it freezes into ice. Pure water freezes at 0 °C (32 °F) and boils at 100 °C (212 °F) at sea level.

Same stuff, different state. Ice, liquid water, and water vapor are all the same thing: water. Only the way its particles move has changed. That's why a melted ice cube is still water you could drink.

Facts checked against NASA Glenn Research Center's page on states of matter and Britannica's entry on states of matter, explained in our own words.

🧪 Try it: The ice-melting race

  1. With a grown-up's help, put one ice cube on each of three small plates.
  2. Place one plate in a sunny window, one on a kitchen counter, and one in a cool, shady spot.
  3. Guess which ice cube will melt first and write your guess down.
  4. Check every 10 minutes. Which melted first? What state is the water in now?

Think about it: Why do you think the ice cubes melted at different speeds? What could you do to make an ice cube last longer?
