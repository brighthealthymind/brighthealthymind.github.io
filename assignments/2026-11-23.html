Friction: Why Do Things Slow Down?

Read the lesson together, take the 5-question quiz, then try the slide test at home.

🛝 Science · Ages 7–10 · About 10 min

The Invisible Force That Slows Things Down

Roll a ball across the floor. It goes for a while, then slows down and stops, even though nobody grabbed it. Why? A force called friction is at work!

Friction happens when two surfaces rub or slide against each other. It always pushes against the way something is moving, so it slows things down.

  • Rough surfaces have more friction. Carpet, grass, and sandpaper are bumpy, so things slow down quickly on them.
  • Smooth surfaces have less friction. Ice, a polished floor, or a playground slide let things glide farther.
  • Pressing harder makes more friction. A heavy box is harder to slide than an empty one.
  • Friction makes heat. Rub your hands together fast. Feel them warm up? That's friction turning motion into heat.

Friction is a helper, too! Without it, you couldn't walk without slipping, hold a pencil, or stop your bike. Shoe tread adds grip, and bike brakes squeeze pads on the wheel to make friction on purpose. Sometimes we want less friction, so we use wheels, oil, or slippery surfaces to help things move easily.

This lesson is written in our own words, based on basic physical science facts shared by trusted science education sources such as NASA and the Smithsonian.

🧦 Try it: The slide test

  1. Ask a grown-up for a hardcover book and a small, flat object like an eraser or a bottle cap.
  2. Prop one end of the book up on two or three other books to make a gentle ramp.
  3. Place the object at the top and let it go. Does it slide, or does it stay put?
  4. Now cover the ramp with a different material, like a towel, a sheet of paper, or aluminum foil, and try again.
  5. Before each try, predict: will it slide faster or slower? Then check if you were right.

Think about it: Which surface had the most friction? Can you think of one place at home or school where friction helps you every day?
