Why Do We Have Seasons?

Read the lesson together, take the 5-question quiz, then try a flashlight experiment to see why some sunlight feels warmer than other sunlight.

🌍 Science · Ages 7–10 · About 10 min

Earth's Big Tilt

In many places, the year moves through four seasons: spring, summer, fall, and winter. Summer is warm with long days, and winter is chilly with short days. But why does this happen? Many people guess it's because Earth gets closer to the Sun in summer. That's a smart guess, but it isn't the reason! Earth is actually a little closer to the Sun in early January, when it's winter in places like the United States.

The real reason is a tilt. Earth spins around an imaginary line called its axis, which runs from the North Pole to the South Pole. That axis isn't straight up and down. It leans over a bit, about 23 degrees. As Earth travels around the Sun, which takes about one year, the tilt keeps pointing the same way in space.

Leaning toward the Sun. For part of the year, the top half of Earth (the Northern Hemisphere) leans toward the Sun. Sunlight hits it more directly, and the days are longer, so it gets more warmth. That's summer there! Half a year later, Earth is on the other side of the Sun, and the top half leans away. Sunlight comes in at a slant and spreads out, days are shorter, and it's winter. Spring and fall happen in between.

Opposite seasons. When the top half of Earth leans toward the Sun, the bottom half (the Southern Hemisphere) leans away. So when it's winter in Canada in December, it's summer in Australia! Some kids there celebrate the December holidays at the beach.

Facts checked against NASA Space Place and National Geographic Education, explained in our own words.

🔦 Try it: Straight light vs. slanted light

  1. Find a flashlight and a sheet of paper. A slightly dim room works best. Ask a grown-up to help.
  2. Hold the flashlight straight above the paper, pointing straight down. Look at the spot of light. Is it small and bright?
  3. Keep the flashlight the same distance away, but tilt it so the light hits the paper at a slant.
  4. Look again. Is the spot bigger? Does it look dimmer?
  5. Draw both spots on another sheet of paper and label one "summer sun" and one "winter sun."

Think about it: When the same light is spread over a bigger spot, does each part of the paper get more light or less? How does that help explain why winter sunshine feels weaker?
