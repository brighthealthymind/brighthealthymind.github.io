How Does Sound Travel? Vibrations You Can Hear

Read the lesson together, take the 5-question quiz, then build a string telephone to hear sound travel.

🔊 Science · Ages 7–10 · About 10 min

Sound Is a Shake You Can Hear

Every sound you hear starts with something vibrating, which means moving back and forth very fast. When you pluck a rubber band, it wiggles. When you talk, small parts in your throat called vocal cords wiggle. Put your fingers gently on your throat and hum. Can you feel the buzz? That buzz is a vibration.

How sound moves. A vibrating thing bumps into the tiny bits of air next to it. Those bits bump into the next bits, and the next, like a line of dominoes passing along a push. This traveling push is called a sound wave. When the wave reaches your ear, it makes your eardrum vibrate too, and your brain turns that into a sound you understand.

Sound needs something to travel through. Sound can move through air, through water, and through solid things like wood, metal, or a wall. It actually moves faster through water than through air, and even faster through many solids. In outer space there is almost no air, so sound cannot travel there. That is why astronauts use radios to talk to each other on a spacewalk.

Loud and soft, high and low. A bigger vibration makes a louder sound, and a smaller one makes a softer sound. A faster vibration makes a higher sound, like a whistle, and a slower vibration makes a lower sound, like a big drum.

Facts checked against the U.S. National Park Service's guide to understanding sound and Britannica's sound article, explained in our own words.

🏠 Try it: Make a string telephone

  1. With a grown-up's help, poke a small hole in the bottom of two paper cups.
  2. Push the ends of a long piece of string (about as long as a room) through the holes and tie a knot inside each cup so it can't slip out.
  3. Each person holds one cup and walks apart until the string is pulled tight.
  4. Take turns: one person talks softly into a cup while the other holds their cup to their ear.
  5. Now let the string go loose, or pinch it with your fingers, and try again. What changes?

Think about it: Why do you think the voice sounds clearer when the string is tight? (Hint: what has to happen to the string for the sound to travel along it?)

Sources: National Park Service: Understanding Sound · Britannica: Sound
