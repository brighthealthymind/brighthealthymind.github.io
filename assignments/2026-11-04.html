Why Is the Sky Blue?

Read the lesson together, take the 5-question quiz, then make a tiny "sky" with milk, water, and a flashlight.

🔬 Science · Ages 7–10 · About 10 min

Blue Light Everywhere

Look up on a clear day. The sky is a bright, soft blue. It is not a painted ceiling, and it is not a mirror of the ocean. The color comes from sunlight meeting Earth's air.

Sunlight is a mix of colors. Even though the Sun looks white or yellow, its light is made of all the rainbow colors traveling together — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and violet. When those colors stay mixed, we see white light.

Air scatters the short waves. Light travels in waves. Blue and violet waves are shorter than red waves. Tiny gas molecules in the atmosphere bounce those short waves around in every direction. Scientists call this kind of bouncing scattering. Because blue light gets scattered the most, it fills the sky and reaches your eyes from all sides.

Why not violet? Violet scatters a lot too, but our eyes notice blue more easily, and some violet is absorbed high up. So the sky we see looks blue, not purple.

Why sunsets turn red and orange. When the Sun is low, its light takes a longer path through the air. Most of the blue light gets scattered away along that path. The longer red and orange waves keep going, so the sky near the Sun glows warm colors.

Ideas checked with NASA Space Place and NOAA NESDIS K–12 explanations of why the sky is blue.

🥛 Hands-on activity · About 15 min

Make a milky sky

You need: a clear glass or jar of water, a drop or two of milk, a flashlight (or phone light), and a darkish room. Ask a grown-up to help with the light.

  1. Shine the flashlight through the clear water from the side. Look from the other side and from above. The beam may look faint or whitish.
  2. Add one or two drops of milk and stir gently. Tiny milk bits act a bit like air particles that scatter light.
  3. Shine the light through again. From the side, the water often looks bluish or cloudy blue — like a miniature sky.
  4. Look straight into the beam from the far end of the glass (do not stare into a bright bulb). It may look more yellow or orange, a little like sunlight at sunset after blue light has been scattered away.
  5. Talk about what you saw: which view looked more like daytime sky, and which felt more like sunset?

Think about it: On a smoky or dusty day the sky can look pale. Extra particles scatter even more light. How is that similar to adding too much milk?
